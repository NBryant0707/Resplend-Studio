Atelier notes

Twelve weeks
of line.

Marc Aubert on the eighty-four days it took to draft every one of the three hundred and twelve components of the Calibre V-12 — twice over, in pencil, by hand.

  • AuthorMarc Aubert
  • DateMarch 2026
  • Reading9 minutes
  • CategoryAtelier notes

The desk

Eighty-four
days.

Two redrawn escapements. One winter. Twelve weeks before the first plate left the atelier for hardening.

There is a drawing board on the second floor that no-one else is permitted to use. It is older than the maison — older, in fact, than the building. The baize is worn through at the elbow, and the lamp above it is hung from a brass swing so that it can be brought close to the paper without casting the draughtsman's own shadow across the line. I have started every calibre I have ever drawn at that board. The Calibre V-12 was begun there on the eleventh of January, in a winter cold enough to make the graphite brittle.

The paper was Arches 90 lb hot-pressed, cut to a half-plate. The pencils were Faber-Castell 9000, in grades 2H through 6B. A movement drawing is not a single line — it is a sequence of lines, each telling a different truth to a different eye, and each demanding a different hardness of lead. The escapement needs the 6B; the going train tolerates 2H; the balance cock is drafted in a wash of HB that hovers somewhere between.

The first sketch of the escapement.

The Swiss lever escapement is a small, vicious mechanism. Twenty-three parts. Each one must clear the pallet fork by a margin that, on the finished wheel, is measured in hundredths of a millimetre. I began, as I always begin, with the pallet fork — the anchor piece, in every sense — drawn at twelve times scale on the half-plate, then redrawn at six times, then again at two. The first sketch took the best part of a day. I drew the pallet stones three times before I was satisfied that the impulse face was tangent to the locking.

The mistake I made, in that first week, was to draw the escape wheel before the lever was right. The teeth on a Swiss lever escape wheel are drawn with a special template — a thin celluloid disk with twenty-four radial teeth scored into it — and once the wheel is on the paper it tends to dictate everything else. I erased that first wheel four times in two days before I understood that the problem was not the wheel. The problem was the lever. I turned the plate upside down on the board and started the lever again, this time without the wheel anywhere in sight.

Anatomy of a re-draft

  • The pallet fork was drawn eleven times before its final geometry was agreed.
  • The escape wheel template was re-cut twice — once for tooth depth, once for the impulse-pin clearance.
  • The balance staff was set aside for two weeks while the spring geometry was re-derived from a 1908 set-square belonging to my predecessor.

The week where nothing worked.

The fifth week of the project, the third week of February, was the worst week of my working life. I had been drawing the going train — barrel, centre wheel, third wheel, fourth wheel, escape wheel — and every proportion I set down looked wrong against the main-plate that was to receive them. The wheels were either too small, in which case the energy curve would collapse by the thirty-sixth hour, or too large, in which case the bridge would not seat. I had the entire train laid out on the paper, in pencil, in india ink, in white correcting fluid, and three times erased down to the baize.

I do not remember much of that week. I remember the lamp, and the temperature — the heating in the atelier failed on the Wednesday, and the radiator under the draughtsman's board gave up a low, unhappy hiss. I remember telephoning Édouard Vasseur, the retired Maître who trained me, on the Thursday morning, and his silence on the line for what seemed like a long time before he said only: "re-draw the main-plate."

The going train closes.

The going train closed, finally, on a Monday morning in late March. I had re-drawn the main-plate at three-quarter scale the previous Friday, and on the Saturday taken it to the hardening house in Carouge to be photographed onto a steel photo-etch. By the Monday the new plate had returned, and the train — barrel, centre, third, fourth, escape — had been re-set against it in pencil. The proportions were right. I knew they were right before I measured them. The eye learns this, eventually.

Three hundred and twelve components.

By the eleventh of April, twelve weeks after the first pencil touched paper, the Calibre V-12 existed on paper in its entirety. Three hundred and twelve components, drawn at every scale the workshop would later require: one-to-one for the cutters, two-to-one for the inspectors, twelve-to-one for the escapement. Each component on its own sheet. Each sheet stamped, dated, and initialled in the lower right-hand corner. The stack — bound in linen tape, the colour of weak tea from a hundred years of handling — is kept in the safe under the main staircase.

The first piece of steel left the atelier for hardening on the fourteenth of April, a Tuesday. The going-train bridge. I carried it, in a small wooden box, the two kilometres to the hardening house myself. It was a cold spring morning. The box weighed almost nothing.

After the drawing

A movement drawn in twelve weeks takes eleven days to assemble by hand. Each of the three hundred and twelve parts is filed, polished, and seated by one watchmaker — the same one, beginning to end. The case is milled from a single billet of titanium over forty-seven hours. The name on the caseback is the name of the watchmaker, not the maison.

The drawing is the first part of a long, slow conversation between the paper and the bench. It is not the easier half of that conversation. I have, occasionally, been asked which half I prefer. The honest answer is that I do not know which is harder. I know only that the drawing must be finished before the bench can begin.
