Drawn by hand in Geneva across twelve weeks, regulated across fourteen days in six positions. The V-12 is the maison's reference calibre — manual wind, single barrel, ±2 seconds per day.

Anatomy

The architecture of three hundred parts.

Six named assemblies compose the V-12. The sequence below follows the path of energy — from the coiled spring, through the going train, to the escapement, and back to the balance.

02

Power train

The going train.

Energy, transmitted. The path from a coiled spring to a ticking escapement, made of five wheels, four pinions, and the patience of a hundred years.

Power

From barrel
to balance.

The mainspring sits coiled inside its barrel — the only source of energy in the calibre. Nine turns at full wind. A torque that does not vary more than twelve per cent across the seventy-two-hour reserve. The spring is greased before assembly with Moebius 8200, a single dab applied at the centre coil with a brass spatula.

From the barrel arbor, the energy passes through a sliding clutch to the centre wheel. The centre wheel is the slowest wheel in the train, completing one revolution every hour. Its single pinion engages the third wheel, whose axis is offset by ninety degrees. From there to the fourth, and at last to the escape wheel — each pivot set in a synthetic ruby, each ruby oiled once, at assembly, with a single stroke of a sable brush dipped in Moebius 9010. The oil is kept in a glass bottle dated the day it was drawn.

At the escapement, the energy meets the lever. Each tooth of the escape wheel is bevelled by hand to a thirty-degree angle, allowing the pallet stones to release the wheel in measured increments. The lever rocks once for each half-oscillation of the balance. The balance returns the energy, holding it briefly between two consecutive locks. The cycle is older than the maison, but the maison has simply drawn it more carefully.

The train is the architecture of patience. It does nothing visible, but nothing happens without it. Each V-12 going train is tested on a Witschi time-grapher across twelve simulated wrist positions before the balance is fitted, and again after.

Regulation

Six positions, fourteen days.

Each V-12 is timed in six positions across fourteen days. The published tolerance is two seconds per day. Most leave the maison closer to one.

Time trial

A reference
clock, sealed.

Each piece is timed dial-up, dial-down, crown-up, crown-down, crown-left, crown-right, and across three intermediate arm positions. The reference clock is a Lemania observatory movement kept in a sealed case at constant temperature and pressure. Two seconds per day is the published tolerance — confirmed in writing on the bench card stapled to the caseback the day the piece leaves regulation.

04

Finishing

The patient surface.

Twenty-eight parts leave the atelier decorated. No surface is left as it left the cutter — each one held for a further hour against boxwood, against a linen lap, against the eye of the polisher.

Anglage: the forty-five-degree chamfer is cut by hand and polished on boxwood against a linen lap. Perlage: circular grain applied to the mainplate in overlapping rings by an abrasive head turned at low speed. Black polish: flat steelwork taken to an optical black, the part held so that the lamp reflects without distortion. Each judge the work by the angle of the light.

The polishing bench is the last in the atelier. Light comes from a single lamp on a counterweighted arm, set to a colour temperature of 3200 kelvin so the surface reads true. The polisher works the chamfer for ten minutes against the boxwood, sets the part down, lifts it again, and again. A single surface can take a full morning before the bench accepts it. The polisher is paid by the piece, not by the hour.

Each finishing pass is recorded on the inside cover of the bench book. The number of the part, the date, the abrasive. The book is not a record of triumph — it is a record of repetition. Twenty-eight parts decorated per calibre, twenty-eight decorative decisions, one outcome that has to be right twice.
