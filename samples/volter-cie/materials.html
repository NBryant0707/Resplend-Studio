Six métiers.

Grade 5 titanium, 18k rose gold, 950 platinum, DLC-coated steel. One enamel. One Mississippi alligator. Selected for the way they resist time, not for the way they advertise it.

01

Metals

Four alloys, three centuries.

Each metal chosen for what it does in twenty years, not what it does in the photograph. The bench reads the file before it.

Grade 5 (Ti-6Al-4V)Aerospace specification, alpha-beta alloy, double vacuum-arc remelted.
Density 4.43 g / cm³Lighter than steel by forty per cent. Heavier than aluminium by sixty.
Hardness 36 HRCAfter heat treatment. Soft enough to mill, hard enough to wear.
Yield 89 % billet-to-caseMilled from one billet across forty-seven hours of cut time.
Wear A soft grey patinaDevelops across eighteen months of wrist. Never polished back.

Aerospace-grade titanium, milled from a single billet. The grade holds a fine edge after tooling and weathers with use into a soft grey patina the case is not polished back from. The alloy is anti-magnetic, anti-corrosive, and quiet under the cuff — softer than steel against the shirt, colder than gold against the skin. Chosen as the standard reference for the weight it does not carry.

Alloy 5N (75 % Au, 22.25 % Cu, 2.75 % Ag)Mixed in-house. Trace zinc added at the third anneal.
Density 15.4 g / cm³Denser than titanium by a factor of three and a half.
Hardness 175 HVAfter cold-rolling. Softer than tool steel, harder than pure gold.
Yield 71 % billet-to-caseHot-struck, then hand-polished across two days per case.
Colour A warm old roseDevelops at the third annealing. Does not fade with handling.

An in-house five-nines alloy, mixed and cold-rolled at the bench. The colour develops at the third annealing and does not fade with handling — each piece is set aside for two weeks on a velvet tray in the safe before it is regulated, so the metal finishes its settling before any timing is recorded. Cast, rolled, and struck entirely at the Geneva atelier. The Swiss hallmark is applied at the caseback wing.

Grade 950 fine (95 % Pt, 5 % Ru)Ru-hardened. Supplied under assay by Johnson Matthey.
Density 21.45 g / cm³The densest of the four. The case sinks; the wrist knows it.
Hardness 135 HVAfter work-hardening at the case flanks.
Yield 64 % billet-to-caseAn hour longer per case than the titanium cut, for the spindle speed.
Wear Patina of fine linesRecorded on the caseback card at each service. Never buffed out.

A 950-grade alloy, the densest of the four. Cases are machined an hour longer per piece to accommodate the cutting speeds the metal requires — and finished entirely by hand, the polisher at bench four working each flank across two evenings. Chosen for the patina it acquires, never for the polish it can be brought back to. The hallmark is struck by hand at the caseback wing.

Base 316L stainlessSupplied as drawn bar by Sandvik, Sweden.
Coating Diamond-like carbonAmorphous carbon, deposited by PVD at 200 °C.
Thickness 2 — 4 µmLess than the diameter of a human hair.
Hardness 80 — 85 HRCComposite reading across substrate and film.
Wear A matte black, indefinitelyThe film does not fade. It does not take the light. It absorbs it.

Stainless steel, polished, then coated after assembly with an amorphous carbon film. The film increases surface hardness fivefold and absorbs light across the visible spectrum. Cases are tested at the salt-spray bench for two hundred hours before assembly, then sealed — assembled, then coated — so the film survives the case opening. The DLC variant is the only edition not signed by hand.

A surface fired at 800 degrees.

Guilloché turned by hand on a Linéaire rose engine. Enamel vitrified across four firings. Indices applied, lume painted between two clamps.

Grand feu enamel

Vitreous enamel, applied in three layers, fired across four passes at eight hundred degrees. Each pass burns off the dust on the bench underneath.

03

Applied indices · lume

Indexes cut from a single sheet of 18k gold by Dr. Richter at his atelier in Pforzheim, applied to the dial with tweezers and set in shellac before firing. The lume — Super-LumiNova, grade BG-W9 — is painted between two clamps so the deposit is laid at the same height on each of the twelve positions. By morning, the dial holds a small blue circle for each hour of the night.

Strap

The alligator, at the bench.

One tannery in Saint-Junien, France. One saddler at Volter since 1973. Forty-two stitches per strap.

The strap

Cut, tanned,
stitched.

The hide is Mississippi alligator — sourced farm by farm from a single tannery in Saint-Junien, France. The flanks only are used; the belly is sold for other work. Each hide is treated with vegetable tannin, no chromium, dyed in one of three colours kept in stock from season to season. The tanning takes six weeks; the hide is then laid on a glass plate for a further two weeks before it is cut.

Jean-Paul Bourquin has cut the Volter straps at his bench in Annemasse since 1973. Two days per strap. He works from a printed pattern pinned to the leather and trims the flank against a steel rule, never freehand. The edges are sanded twice — at two hours, then again at twenty — and the lin thread is waxed by drawing it three times through a block of beeswax.

Forty-two stitches per strap. Eight adjustment holes, the centre one marked with a small dot of lacquer so the buckle sits true. The buckles are cut and polished in-house from the same bar of metal as the case, so the strap and the case wear together and patina together, a single object on the wrist.

Each strap is delivered in a flax-lined pouch with the offcuts returned to the tannery. The maison does not sell the strap separately. It is included with the piece, fitted at regulation.
