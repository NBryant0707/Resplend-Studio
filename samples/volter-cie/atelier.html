Maison / The Atelier

Twelve benches, north light.

The Geneva workshop on Rue du Rhône. Twelve benches, north light, and the four named stages of making a movement.

  • Established1912
  • BenchesTwelve
  • MaterialGrade 5 titanium
  • CityGeneva
01

The building

North light on Rue du Rhône.

The workshop occupies a third floor in the old town. The work is paced by the angle of the sun and by the hour of regulation. The room has not changed in fifty years.

The atelier

A long room with twelve benches.

The maison occupies the third floor of a stone building at 14 Rue du Rhône, Geneva. The atelier was refitted in 1968 by the architect Pierre Braunschvig, who stripped the partitions and left the original ashlar exposed. Twelve benches now line the bay windows. The glass faces due north. The light is steady from ten until four — the hours during which any measurement of tolerance is taken.

The floor is oak, laid in 1968 by the parquet firm Berger of Carouge and waxed twice a year. Each bench bears a brass plate — the holder's name, the year of their appointment, and the year of their retirement. Marc Aubert's plate bears the date 1994. The plates of the first bench-holders from 1912 are now in the library. The original loupes hang in the corridor by the door.

The vault lies behind the last door on the right. It holds the components in progress — baseplates, balance wheels, hairsprings, dials in transit — between stages of hand assembly. Temperature is held at 19°C, ±0.5. Relative humidity is held between forty-five and fifty-five percent. The door is opened by Marc Aubert, Maître Horloger, or by appointment.

A second room houses the library: catalogues from the Geneva Observatory, dossiers of discontinued movements, the working notebooks of five head watchmakers, and the assay files dating to 1912. Nothing is lent from this room. Visitors are received there on Thursdays, between two and four.

02

The four stages

From drawing to caseback.

Each V-12 movement passes through four named stages. Each has a duration. None is shortened.

I

The drawing board

Each reference begins on paper. The marker holds a soft pencil and draws the components freehand at scale 12:1, then redraws them at scale 1:1 against the working plate. Three hundred and twelve parts. The drawing takes twelve weeks. No component is committed to manufacture until the marker has signed the sheet. Twelve watches are made each year per reference; none of them shares a working drawing with another. The drawings are filed in the library under the reference number, and kept indefinitely.

II

Case milling

The case blank is cut from a single billet of Grade 5 titanium. Forty-seven hours of milling reduces the blank to the case in sub-millimetre increments. The tooling is changed by hand fifteen times across the run. The satin finish is applied afterwards — by a finisher at Bench 03, with a wooden lap and a paste of diamantine, in unbroken strokes that follow the curve of the case. No two cases share the exact grain of the satin; this is one of the more easily observed marks of the maison. The case is delivered to assembly between the eighteenth and twenty-second day of the run.

III

Hand assembly

A single watchmaker assembles each piece, from start to seal. The assembly takes eleven working days. Across those days the watchmaker sets the components in their seat — barrel, going train, escapement, balance — oils each jewel with the maison's own synthetic, and the watch is closed. On the last afternoon the caseback is engraved with the watchmaker's name in the maison's hand. The engraver has been trained in-house. No piece leaves the atelier without a signature. If a watchmaker is unavailable, the piece waits for them.

IV

Regulation

Once sealed, the watch passes to the regulator. It is worn across six positions for fourteen days. The allowable tolerance is ±2 seconds per day. Anything wider is returned to the bench for re-adjustment; nothing is sent out. The regulator's mark is added to the caseback beneath the watchmaker's mark, in smaller letters, after the run is complete. Regulation is the last act of making. The piece is then returned to Marc Aubert for sign-off before it is boxed for delivery.

04

Tools

Four machines of the bench.

Specialist tools for four kinds of hand. Each is owned, serviced, and used by one watchmaker only.

02

Jacot tool

A pivoting polisher for the steel pivots of balance staffs, escape wheels, and pallet stones. The maison's two jacot tools date from 1929; both are still in service. A pivot is held against a chamois lap charged with diamantine; the lap rotates at twelve turns a minute. A good pivot reflects a single point of light.

03

Timing machine

A Witschi Proofmaster S, purchased in 1998 and recalibrated every two years at the company's atelier in Grenchen. The watch is read on six positions — dial up, dial down, crown up, crown down, crown left, crown right — for fourteen days. The result is filed against the serial number.

Witschi · 1998Six positions

04

Dust cabinet

A laminar-flow enclosure, ISO class 7, installed in 2011. The atmosphere is filtered to ten thousand particles per cubic metre. Components under one milligram are weighed, oiled, and seated inside the cabinet, with the watchmaker's hands through neoprene gauntlets.
