Maison / Heritage

One hundred and fourteen years.
One bench.

From 1912 the maison has occupied the same three rooms above the Rue du Rhône. The work has not changed — only the season the watchmaker's window is open.

01

The years

Eight entries from the bench.

Eight entries from the maison's archive. Each drawn from the bench ledger, the Geneva trade registry, or the regulation card that travelled with a completed calibre.

1912

A bench on the Rue du Rhône

Henri Voltier, twenty-six years old, leaves the atelier of a senior Geneva régleur and takes a one-room bench on the third floor above the Rue du Rhône. The lease — a single page, still in the vault — names no partners. It sets out the rent in Swiss francs and the condition that the window be kept open in summer. The first bench makes eight watches in its first year. Six are pocket pieces for the Geneva trade. Two are wristlets, on request. None yet bear a maison name.

1927

First in-house escapement

Fifteen years in, the maison files its first escapement of its own making — a lever escapement with a steel pallet, drawn by Henri Voltier and finished by his second apprentice, Émile Borel. The mainspring barrel remains outsourced until 1931. The escapement is produced in batches of twenty and is signed on the lower face of the bridge with the house's hallmarks: V&C in a lozenge, the Geneva shield, and the year of completion. The plate ledger for 1927 has twenty-two lines. None are repeated.

1944

The wartime years

During the war years the atelier is lent to the production of instrument timers for the Federal Ordnance. The work is precise, signed off, and held against the maison's tax. The bench does not stop. A small quantity of wristwatches is produced for the Swiss officer trade — austere dials, no indices, a single luminous pip at twelve. They are catalogued in 1944 under the single entry "Officer series". None are numbered. A handful remain in private hands. The maison does not speak of them today.

1961

The Observatory chronometer trials

From 1961 the maison enters the Neuchâtel Observatory chronometer trials for the first time. It is a six-position, fourteen-day classification. The first submission places twenty-fourth. The seventh, in 1964, places second. The maison never enters again after 1967. The internal note, signed by Henri Voltier's son Jean, reads: «The bench knows its rate. We have nothing to prove in Neuchâtel.» Regulation then becomes the house's own. The card used — still in use — is the maison's.

1974

The quartz crisis

The quartz crisis closes two of Geneva's smaller maisons in 1974. Voltier & Cie's order book empties by November. The maison's eight watchmakers are not laid off. They are reassigned to the repair of older calibres — a programme funded by the founders' private reserves. The maison survives on the repair bench for thirty-one months. It is the only period in which the maison has produced nothing new. The last repair of the period, a 1948 pocket watch, returns to its owner in March 1977.

1989

The Aubert family acquires the maison

On the death of Jean Voltier in 1989, the maison is acquired by the Aubert family of La Chaux-de-Fonds. The terms are not disclosed. The single published condition is that the maison keep its name, its bench, and its rule of twelve. Philippe Aubert becomes the maison's third director. The Aubert family brings capital. It does not bring direction. The maison's archive — drawings, plates, regulator cards — is not moved. It remains above the Rue du Rhône.

2011

Marc Aubert, Maître Horloger

Marc Aubert, son of Philippe, is awarded the title of Maître Horloger by the Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie after twelve years at the bench. He has been the maison's lead regulator since 2003. The title is conferred only on practising watchmakers of more than ten years' standing. The maison's prior Maître Horloger, Émile Borel, retired in 1979. Between the two, twenty-eight years elapsed without the title. The maison's regulation card is signed by Marc Aubert on every completed calibre.

2024

Calibre V-12 leaves the regulation bench

Calibre V-12 leaves the regulation bench in November 2024 after twelve weeks of drawing. The calibre is the maison's twelfth reference in one hundred and twelve years. It contains 312 components, of which 188 are signed by hand. The first twelve pieces — Reference 12.04 — leave the atelier in February 2025. The maison's twelfth edition is offered to the register first. The bench is closed for cleaning the following Monday.

03

What was kept

The decision not to industrialise.

Two decisions, taken fifty years apart, that have governed the maison's scale since the bench was opened.

The first decision

Never to
industrialise.

Henri Voltier was offered a workshop expansion in 1923, two floors below the bench, on the same staircase. The terms were generous. The expansion would have allowed the maison to employ thirty watchmakers and to produce in series. The offer was declined. The maison has occupied the same three rooms since.

The decision was not cast as a principle at the time. It was recorded in a single sentence in the bench ledger: «The light is right here. We shall not move.» It has governed the maison's scale ever since. The atelier cannot produce more than it does without relocating, and the maison has resolved not to relocate. Output is therefore fixed by the number of benches the rooms can hold.

The consequences are visible in the catalogue. The maison's twelve references in one hundred and fourteen years are not a slow programme — they are the programme of a single bench operating at full capacity.

The second decision

Twelve pieces.
No more.

The rule of twelve is attributed to Émile Borel, and dated by the maison to 1934. The rule is uncodified — it is observed, not written. A reference is produced in twelve pieces, numbered in the order of completion, and never repeated. The maison does not produce a "second series". A reference that sells out is sold out.

The justification given by Borel, in an internal note later transcribed by Jean Voltier, is a single line: «A watch that is repeated becomes a product. A watch that is not becomes a record.»

The rule has been honoured through the quartz crisis, through three changes of ownership, and through the development of Calibre V-12. The maison's twelfth reference in one hundred and twelve years is offered to the register first. The edition will not be repeated.

04

The mark

The maison's hallmark.

A note on the maison's hallmark, struck on the bridge of every completed calibre since 1927.

Poinçon de Genève

A mark of
workmanship.

The Poinçon de Genève — the Geneva Hallmark — was established in 1886 by the Canton of Geneva. It is not a mark of origin. It is a mark of workmanship: a watch must be assembled, regulated, and tested within the canton, to a published standard of finish. The standard is set by the Canton and revised annually.

Voltier & Cie's hallmark has comprised three elements since 1927. The V&C monogram in a lozenge, struck on the upper bridge of the calibre. The Geneva shield, struck on the lower bridge. The year of completion, struck in italic numerals beside the shield. The mark is struck on the movement, not on the case. The case carries the maison's engraving only.

A movement without the maison's hallmark is not a Voltier, regardless of whose case it sits in. A case without the maison's engraving is not a Voltier, regardless of whose movement is inside. The two marks together constitute the maison's signature. They are applied by hand, by the Maître Horloger, on the day the calibre leaves the regulation bench.
